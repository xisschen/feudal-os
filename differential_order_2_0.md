# Differential Order 2.0: From Bloodline Ripples to Algorithmic Ripples

## *A personal observation on "your ripples are still there — it's just who sits in which ring is now determined by algorithms"*

---

In 1947, Fei Xiaotong threw a pebble into the water in *From the Soil*.

"Our pattern is not like distinct bundles of firewood tied neatly together, but rather like the ripples spreading outward in circles from a stone dropped into water. Each person is the center of the concentric circles pushed out by their social influence."

This is *chaxu geju* — the differential order. Chinese social relations are not egalitarian and group-based — they are graded and concentric. The innermost ring holds parents and children; the next, siblings; beyond them, clan members, neighbors, strangers. Each ring carries different moral obligations — filial piety toward parents, fraternal deference toward brothers, trustworthiness toward friends. You don't need to ask "what should I do for him?" You look at which ring he occupies.

This model has dominated the Chinese sociological imagination for eighty years. Its core assumption: the sorting logic of the ripples is **blood.** The closer to the center, the thicker the blood.

In 2026, this assumption no longer fully holds.

It's not that the differential order has disappeared. It's that the sorting logic has changed.

This paper is not about "algorithms creating information cocoons." Pariser's filter bubbles and Sunstein's echo chambers have been discussed for two decades. This paper is about something else: the cocoons created by algorithms happen to be structurally isomorphic with the differential order. Algorithms did not create a new social structure. Algorithms replaced blood as the content loaded into a structure that has existed for three thousand years.

The deep structure of the differential order does not change — self at the center, concentric ripples spreading outward by intimacy, each ring with its own moral obligations. What changes is who occupies each ring, and the basis on which they are sorted. Blood was the first content loaded into this structure. Friends were the second. Algorithms are the third. Fei's original concept has not been overthrown — it has been fitted with a new engine.

## 1. Three Shocks: How the Ripples Shattered

The bloodline ripple required one physical condition: the people mapped by blood relations had to remain within long-term accessible range. Your father lived in the next village, your uncle lived in town, your cousin farmed the same field. Blood and space overlapped. The ripples were drawn along bloodlines and drawn on the ground.

Three things simultaneously destroyed this physical prerequisite.

**Urbanization.** 290 million rural migrant workers moved their bodies from village to city. Blood relations remain — but your father is in Jiangxi, you're in Dongguan, your cousin is in Shenzhen. Space no longer overlaps with blood. The "nearest ring" of the ripple is no longer physically present.

**The internet.** When WeChat saturated China in the 2010s, social interaction was liberated from geographic constraint. You deal with someone not because you live next door — but because you're in the same chat group. Blood relations have a position in your contact list, but compared to the people you talk to every day, that position is no longer necessarily at the top.

**Algorithmic recommendation.** After 2018, short-video recommendation engines began actively determining "who appears in your field of vision." Douyin pushes you toward people with similar behavior. Kuaishou's same-city page inserts local strangers' updates into your feed. WeChat group chat ordering is decided by recent activity — no regard for blood, only interaction density.

The cumulative result: the bloodline ripple is still there, but it is no longer the most active interaction ring in your life.

## 2. The Algorithmic Ripple: A New Sorting Logic

Algorithms did not come to destroy the differential order. They came to rebuild it.

The differential order has two core features: (1) self-centered, concentric outward propagation; (2) different behavioral norms for different rings. Neither feature has disappeared in the algorithmic age — only who sits in each ring, and what this ring's norms are, are now partially defined by algorithms.

Douyin's recommendation system is an engineering implementation of the differential order:

- The innermost ring — people you follow, people you repeatedly interact with. The algorithm identifies these as "strong ties" and pushes them at high frequency. These people are not necessarily your relatives. They may be in the same line of business, like the same game, follow the same streamer. Blood carries no special weight here. Behavioral similarity replaces blood proximity as the sorting basis of the first ripple.

- The second ring — people the algorithm thinks you "ought to know." Same city, same income band, same consumption habits. Not because you have history with them. Because the algorithm placed them in your ripple. It predicts you will be mutually interested, and then it makes the prediction a reality.

- The third ring — the content pool. The algorithm tests your response: did you pause on this kind of content? Like it? Share it? Dwell time is the new differential order's "ritual." It determines your position within the ripple.

Fei Xiaotong said "every person is the center of their own social relations" — this sentence is more literal in the algorithmic age than in the agrarian age. Your Douyin homepage is the map of your ripples. Your Kuaishou same-city page is the administrative district of your concentric circles. Your WeChat group chat ordering is the real-time ranking of your differential order. The center is still you. The center has never been so secure. Only the content of the rings is no longer determined by the clan.

## 3. The "Ritual" of the Algorithmic Ripple: A New Normative System

The differential order is not only "who is close to me" — it is also "what I should do for each ring." In the bloodline ripple, this behavioral code is *li* — ritual propriety: filial piety, fraternal deference, loyalty, trustworthiness, each ring with its corresponding moral duty.

The algorithmic ripple has its own *li.* It is not called *li* — but the function is identical:

| Bloodline Ripple (Fei) | Algorithmic Ripple (2020s) |
|---|---|
| Filial piety — absolute obligation to parents | Traffic — absolute obligation to fans ("following you is giving you face") |
| Fraternal deference — obedience to brothers | Likes, shares, comments — mutual aid to same-ring members ("mutual follows and mutual likes are basic etiquette") |
| Trustworthiness — fidelity obligation to friends | Follow — the ritual act of establishing a relationship ("follow so you won't get lost") |
| Expulsion — excluding rule-breakers from the ring | Unfollow / block — excluding rule-breakers from the ring |
| Taboo naming — prohibition on authority names | Shadow-ban / mute — hiding violating content |
| Positional name — "you are of this branch" | Identity tag — "you are this content creator's fan" |

The algorithmic ripple's operation is not "normless" — the norms have already grown; they just aren't called *li* yet. A Douyin user knows "mutual follows must like each other's posts — not liking is a loss of face." This is the same cognitive structure as a villager knowing "when someone in the clan has an event, you must attend — not attending means you don't understand *li.*" No one taught it. You soaked in the system long enough, and it formatted you automatically.

This is precisely the underlying mechanism of the differential order: rules are written into the cognitive substrate, no longer perceived as "rules," but perceived as "the way things just are." Algorithms do not enforce this ritual through violence — they use push notifications, ranking, and shadow-banning to ensure that every person, in the process of using the system, spontaneously learns the behavioral codes of the new differential order. (The Formatics research framework, to which this paper belongs, calls this process "formatting" — the process by which social norms are written into an individual's cognitive substrate and run as default protocols.)

## 4. Friend Society: Naming the Transitional State

But the algorithmic ripple has not yet fully taken over. The bloodline ripple has shattered, the algorithmic ripple has not yet stabilized — there is a transitional state in between.

That transitional state is friend society.

There is a good line: friends are the family you choose yourself. Bloodline family is assigned at birth — assigned to you the way a "name" is assigned, not chosen. Friends you choose yourself. The power of choice moved from the system to the individual — but the structure after choosing — concentric intimacy, mutual-aid obligations, ring after ring of ripples — looks exactly like the bloodline ripple.

"Friend" here is not a private emotional concept. It is a functional mutual-aid network. Once blood relations have been geographically scattered, who helps you get things done in the county town? Not your cousin — he's working in another province. It's the "friend" you know — whose cousin works in the education bureau, whose classmate works at the hospital, whose hometown acquaintance is at the police station. This network can continue to provide basic survival functions after the clan's tentacles have snapped: job referrals, emergency loans, connections for getting a child into a school.

County-town high-cost social obligations run on this. A banquet sets thirty tables, twenty of them "friends." It's not that there are fewer relatives — it's that the relatives' geographic distribution does not permit them to respond instantly to your mutual-aid needs. Friends fill the physical vacuum in the inner rings of the ripple.

This is the formatting migration Chinese society is undergoing: from bloodline formatting (clan system) to algorithmic formatting (recommendation engines), passing through friend formatting (functional mutual-aid networks). The three are not linear replacements — they are superimpositions. A living person is now simultaneously formatted by three differential orders:

- Returning to the village for Spring Festival — the bloodline ripple activates: you must go to the ancestral hall, must toast the elders
- Handling daily matters in the county town — the friend ripple activates: you must maintain favors, must contribute your share
- Scrolling on your phone — the algorithmic ripple activates: you must like, must follow, must dwell where the algorithm wants you to dwell

Three differential orders stacked together. Each has its own center (all you), its own ripple sorting, its own behavioral codes. Switching is automatic, unconscious. You don't need to think "which set of ritual should I use now?" — your body already knows. This is the completed state of formatting: not one person living within three sets of rules, but three sets of rules living within one person.

## 5. Returning to Fei Xiaotong

When Fei Xiaotong wrote about the differential order in 1947, the question he faced was: why does Chinese society organize itself not as egalitarian citizen-group relations, but as private, concentric, ring-by-ring relations?

His answer: because the basic organizational unit of agrarian society is the bloodline group, not the individual.

Eighty years later, agrarian society is gone. But the differential order remains. Only the organizational unit has shifted from blood to data, from clan to algorithm. The deep structure of traditional Chinese society — the self-centered graded relationship — has not been dissolved by modernization. It has been fitted with a new engine by modernization.

Fei Xiaotong in 1947 did not know about algorithms. But he saw the fundamental thing: Chinese social relations are not egalitarian and not group-based — they are self-centered, graded, outward-rippling concentric circles. This structure is harder than any specific social form.

Blood was merely the first content loaded into this structure.
Friends were the second.
Algorithms are the third.

What comes next, no one knows. But the structure itself — the differential order — will not change. Whatever formatting comes, it will load whatever content. The center is forever you. The ripples forever push outward. The behavioral codes of each ring are forever waiting for you to be formatted.

Seen from the underlying protocol of Formatics, the differential order has survived eighty years unbroken because it is responsible for only one link in the five-character drive shaft of name → position → ritual → action → reality: position. It does not name you (name), does not produce your final identity (reality), does not generate behavioral norms (ritual). It does exactly one thing: places each person into the correct ring of the ripple according to some sorting logic. Blood was the first sorting engine for position. Friends were the second. Algorithms are the third. The engine changed; the function of position did not. The function of position is so fundamental that as long as human society still needs to "distinguish near from far, close from distant," the differential order will always have something to say.

## Coda: Fei Xiaotong's Pebble

Fei Xiaotong threw a pebble into the water, and the ripples pushed outward.

In 2026, the water is still there. The pebble has been replaced, again and again. The center of the ripples is still you. Only the thrower of the pebble — now it's not just you. The algorithm is throwing stones too.
